What can microtransit
do for public transit agencies?
VIa
Microtransit is every transit agency's ally in
upgrading the customer experience, growing ridership,
and improving efficiency.
Aggregating riders going from different origins to different destinations in real time - and
routing vehicles dynamically — creates operational possibilities that were unimaginable only
five years ago:
Grow ridership of existing
fixed-route service- bus lines,
bus rapid transit, light rail, and
subways — by solving the first
and last mile problem.
Upgrading legacy dial-a-ride
and paratransit services by
making them on-demand
while, at the same time, using
vehicles more efficiently.
Optimizing the
coverage/frequency trade-off
by replacing underperforming
fixed-route service with
real-time demand response
and using the savings to
increase the frequency of
fixed-route service in
high-demand corridors.
The question, therefore, is how best to integrate microtransit into an agency's existing service
portfolio. Since transit agencies are public organizations that are charged with operating safe,
equitable, and reliable service on behalf of their communities, before changing the status quo
an agency needs to be confident that a new service will strengthen its ability to serve the
public, not weaken it. A transit agency, unlike a private transportation operator, doesn't have
the luxury of experimenting with many different ideas until it finds the right service model; an
agencys decision-makers need to make the most informed decision possible about the right
service before the first vehicle is dispatched.

Finding the right partner
When an agency considers whom to work
with, there are a few considerations:
On-demand and dynamic
The most successful microtransit services are on-demand —
customers get rides when they want them — and fully
dynamic, meaning there are no static routes or schedules.
Powering a flexible service that is continuously optimized in
real time is a big technical challenge.
Moreover, it's also important to confirm that the technology
has been proven in similar markets in terms of population
density, origin-destination patterns, and traffic
characteristics - most microtransit algorithms are
configured to function well in only one specific kind of
market.
Scalability
It's one thing to power a dial-a-ride service with a few
vehicles; it's another thing entirely to power a microtransit
service at scale with dozens or even hundreds of vehicles
delivering hundreds or thousands of rides every day.
The ability to do the former, moreover, usually doesn't transfer
to the latter - as the number of vehicles and rides increase,
the number of possible rider/vehicle assignments and routing
options grow exponentially, which requires both an algorithm
and automated service management tools built to handle
scale.

=== page 6 of 26 ===
Built for public transit
Can the microtransit partner provide product features that
are specific to a transit agency's public mission? Will people
who go to recurring medical appointments be able to
pre-schedule rides for themselves and, if necessary, for
caretakers too? Similarly, will people in wheelchairs be able to
request wheelchair accessible vehicles?
What about low-income customers — will they be able
to access the service if they don't have smartphones or
credit cards?
Access to best practices
Technology alone doesn't a successful microtransit service
make. Since transit agencies have historically configured
service around fixed routes and schedules, even the sawiest
service planners and operations managers don't have
experience running real-time on-demand service with
dynamically-routed vehicles.
The best service plans are just academic if theyre not
effectively implemented and managed. What's the best way to
provide real-time customer service? What's the most
cost-effective way to acquire new riders?
Trying to reinvent the microtransit playbook is risky (as some
agencies have learned) - choosing a microtransit partner
with extensive experience launching, scaling, and optimizing
service could be the difference between success and failure.
Powerful data science tools
What kind of analytics and reporting services
does the partner provide?
Even a small service can generate millions of data points;
distilling actionable intelligence from data sets on that scale
requires powerful data science tools, analytical expertise, and
deep domain experience.

Data-driven service design and
computer modeling reduces risk
VIa
Via's microtransit experts have thought through service planning challenges in virtually every
kind of environment: big cities, small towns, resort communities, car-oriented suburbs, and
even rural areas. After providing more than twenty-five million microtransit rides and collecting
billions of data points, they know what it takes to analyze a new market and scope out a
successful service. Via's data scientists and operations professionals work with transit agencies
to de-risk their microtransit investments by getting answers to key questions:
• How can microtransit most effectively complement existing service?
• What are the most attractive use-cases for current customers?
• How can microtransit service attract new riders?
• How many vehicles are needed to achieve a compelling quality of service
(QoS), measured in terms of pickup and trip times?
• How much should riders pay per trip?
• What would success look like in terms of total ride volume, service productivity
(passengers/vehicle/hour), cost-effectiveness, and QoS?
Via's methodology combines an agency's available ridership and performance data, mapping
and demographic analysis, computer simulation using Via's real-time passenger aggregation
and dynamic vehicle routing algorithm, and the practical experience Via has accumulated
modeling and implementing microtransit around the world. At the end of this interactive
exercise, the agency will have a vetted service model — built upon Via's industry-leading best
practices — that is ready to pilot.

Step 1 | General Assessment
The agency describes issues or shortcomings with
existing services, offers preliminary ideas about how
microtransit can be integrated into its network, and
provides a few basic descriptive inputs, like the number of
vehicles available (including their capacities) and the
number of budgeted vehicle hours. If this information isn't
available, Via works with the agency's team to develop the
stabilizing assumptions needed to scope and model
microtransit service.
Step 2 | Analysis
Via layers technical inputs and assumptions about customer
expectations onto the basic vision for the service in order to
identify the most attractive use-case(s) for microtransit. This
entails analyzing the local physical environment, sorting
areas by land use-type, size, and density, and mapping
points of interest according to their functional roles for
residents.

VIA'S CREDENTIALS
Austin's Capital Metropolitan Transportation Authority's Pickup service, a Via-powered
microtransit offering, showcases how forward-thinking transit agencies can reengineer
paratransit and dial-a-ride for the benefit of their communities.
Before working with Via, Capital Metro operated a conventional deviated-fixed route service
with low ridership. With the hands-on support of Via's microtransit experts, in June 2017
Capital Metro launched a flexible, on-demand service that utilizes its dispatchers, vehicles, and
operators in an entirely new way. Pickup riders can now request rides anywhere within the
service area directly from a custom-built app as well as through a dispatcher, and get picked
up within minutes in wheelchair accessible cutaway buses. And to support the unbanked,
Pickup was designed to accept cash fares.
Pickup has quickly achieved previously unattainable levels of service quality for customers and
operational efficiency for the Authority.
