Palo Alto agreed this week to continue Palo Alto Link for another 12 months. Courtesy City of Palo Alto.
Palo Alto Link, a rideshare program that made its debut in 2023, will remain in operations for another year
despite widespread concerns that the service may not be viable in the long run.
The program functions much like a cheaper version of Uber and Lyft in that it allows residents to use an app
to summon rides and get dropped off at most city locations, with exceptions in open space preserves. The
council had initially approved Palo Alto Link in December 2022, and the program has gotten by through a
combination of city funding, grants and a contribution from Stanford Research Park, which allows employees
to get first- and last-mile service to transit services.
But with the city staring at a budget deficit and the Santa Clara VTA grants coming to an end, members of the
City Council debated this week whether it still makes sense to keep Palo Alto Link running. Over the past
year, the council tried to limit its expense by reducing the hours and increasing the rates on the service, which
at $4 per ride is still far cheaper than other services.
=== page 3 ===
According to Nathan Baird, program manager at the Office of Transportation, the cost cutting has somewhat
diminished the service levels. Wait times have gone up from 10 to 15 minutes when the program made its
debut to about 18 to 22 minutes today.
It has also become more common for people to be notified that there are no seats available for them. In the
past, the notice came up about 10% of the time. Now, it’s more like 20%.
“One of five times you get a message, ‘There’s no seat available to you now,’” Baird told the council on June
17.
And despite the recent expense reductions, the program is still requiring significant city subsidies. An average
trip used to cost the city between $27 and $35; today it’s $23 to $30.
In considering the city budget this week, some council members suggested that it might be time for the city to
cut its losses. Council member Keith Reckdahl suggested that any further service reductions and fare
increases could make Palo Alto Link unattractive compared to Uber.
“If we’re saying that we can’t subsidize it as much, we have to reduce our subsidy, the program will fall apart
because nobody will take it, because it will cost the same as uber and not be as prompt,” Reckdahl said during
the June 17 discussion.
Council members generally agreed that it may be time to wind the program down. They also concurred that
it’s critical to ensure that some of the more vulnerable users – including seniors and residents with disabilities
– need time to transition to something else before the program is halted. Giving the program another year will
give the city a chance to evaluate other services and, if needed, transition to those.
“There are users who treat this as a lifeline or near that. They are the elderly who may not be able to drive,
there are youth who don’t drive yet … and other people who are disabled and may need it,” Burt said. “Going
forward, if we don’t have a sustainable budget, I’d like to make sure that we’re already thinking about what a
reduced project would be.”
To that end, the council voted 5-0, with Vice Mayor Vicki Veenker recusing and Council member Greer Stone
absent, to sign a $1.1-million contract with Nomad Transit to keep Palo Alto Link running for another year.
Mayor Ed Lauing suggested that it may not be worthwhile to extend the program beyond that.
“We’ve seen the results so far. It serves well those people that it serves but we just can’t afford it once the
grants are gone,” Lauing said. “Why go down that road when we know the road is going to stop at a cliff

Even with the financial challenges, there remains some hope for the program, which is administered by
Nomad Transit and consists of 10 vehicles. The city has applied for two grants that could help keep Palo Alto
Link running while reducing the local subsidy, Baird said. Palo Alto officials are expecting to learn this fall
whether they will receive these grants, which may help determine what the program will look like and how
long it will remain in operation.
The council had initially flirted with the idea of limiting the contract extension to a six-month term to limit
expenses. It swerved from that idea after staff argued that it will need more time to find an alternative.
“Even the evaluation of alternative services is going to take time,” said Assistant City Manager Kiely Nose.
“Your decision-making on the subsidies is going to take time, and then the transition.”
While the council agreed to advance the staff request for more time, Reckdahl said he is “pessimistic” about
the program’s future.
“I want to give you time to think about it. But I don’t want to give you time hoping for a Hail Mary,”
Reckdahl said. “If we don’t think it’s realistic maybe we should cut our losses. But if you think there’s a
realistic chance, I’d be inclined to give you the realistic chance.”
